# Vídeo 01 — Toy Aisle Ghosts — roteiro (EN)
**Title:** What Happened to Toys"R"Us? The Store Every Kid Begged to Visit
**Target:** ~1.750 palavras · ~11,5 min a ~150 wpm (tempos aproximados; ajustar depois da narração) · voz: grave, calma, documentário

## 0:00 — Hook
[VISUAL: closed store at night, sign flickering off; kinetic word "GONE".]
On March fifteenth, twenty eighteen, Toys"R"Us announced it would close every single store in America. One week later, the man who built it died. He was ninety four {F1}{F2}.

[VISUAL: 735 red pins vanish from a US map, one by one.]
Seven hundred and thirty five stores. Around thirty thousand jobs {F3}. The place every kid begged to visit, gone in a single spring.

[VISUAL: bright new store opening, ribbon cutting, kids running in. Kinetic: "120 NEW STORES".]
And now, eight years later, something strange is happening. This Christmas, Toys"R"Us is opening one hundred and twenty new stores across the country {F4}.

[VISUAL: empty shopping cart rolling down a dark toy aisle.]
So what actually killed it? It wasn't Amazon. Not really. It wasn't Walmart either. The real answer is a deal most people have never heard of, signed in a boardroom in two thousand five. And by the end of this video, you'll see why the company was doomed thirteen years before the last store went dark.

## 0:55 — A crib store that became a toy empire
[VISUAL: 1940s Washington street, small shop under a bicycle repair sign. Caption: "Washington, D.C. — 1948".]
The story starts in nineteen forty eight, in Washington, D.C. A twenty five year old war veteran named Charles Lazarus opened a small shop below his father's bicycle store {F5}. It wasn't a toy store. It sold baby furniture. Cribs. Strollers. He called it Children's Bargain Town.

[VISUAL: postwar baby boom: rows of prams in a park, newspaper headline style.]
It was perfect timing. Soldiers were coming home, and America was having babies like never before.

[VISUAL: a crib, then a pile of toys; kinetic: "ONCE" vs "AGAIN AND AGAIN".]
But Lazarus noticed a problem. A family buys a crib once. Maybe twice. Then they never come back. Toys were different. Toys broke. Kids grew out of them. And every birthday, every Christmas, the parents came back for more.

[VISUAL: 1950s supermarket aisles, shopping carts.]
So he did something nobody else was doing. He looked at the new supermarkets popping up across America, with their giant aisles and shopping carts, and asked a simple question. Why not a supermarket, but for toys?

[VISUAL: 1957 store front; the logo letters assemble, the R flips backwards. Caption: "1957".]
In nineteen fifty seven, he opened his first store that sold only toys {F2}. He gave it a name that sounded like a kid wrote it, with the R turned backwards. Toys"R"Us.

## 2:10 — The golden age
[VISUAL: warehouse-sized store, aisles to the ceiling; slow push down an aisle.]
The formula was simple. Huge stores. Every toy you could imagine, stacked to the ceiling. Low prices. And a giraffe.

[VISUAL: friendly cartoon giraffe silhouette (not the real mascot art); label "Geoffrey".]
Geoffrey the giraffe had started out in the ads for that old baby furniture shop {F5}. Now he became the face of an empire.

[VISUAL: US map filling with red dots, decade counter rolling 1960 → 1990.]
Through the seventies and eighties, Toys"R"Us spread across the country. It became what business people call a category killer. Small toy shops simply couldn't compete. For roughly fifteen years, it was the biggest toy seller in America {F6}.

[VISUAL: 90s TV, kid singing in a toy aisle; kinetic: "I DON'T WANNA GROW UP".]
And it had a jingle every kid in America could sing. I don't wanna grow up, I'm a Toys"R"Us kid.

[VISUAL: kid pressing face to a glass case of video games; a pick-up ticket.]
If you were a kid in the eighties or nineties, you remember the feeling. The video game aisle, where you grabbed a paper ticket and took it to the register. The bikes hanging from the ceiling. The wish list you circled in the catalog. It wasn't just a store. It was the place where Christmas started.

[VISUAL: Times Square at night, giant glowing storefront, indoor Ferris wheel. Caption: "Times Square — 2001".]
In two thousand one, the company opened its crown jewel. A four story flagship in Times Square, with a sixty foot Ferris wheel inside and a giant animatronic T-rex {F7}.

[VISUAL: dark aisle; kinetic: "BUT…"]
But by then, the empire was already cracking. And the first crack came from a place nobody in the toy business was watching.

## 3:45 — The first crack: Walmart
[VISUAL: discount superstore exterior, 90s parking lot; price tags slashing down.]
In the nineties, Walmart and Target figured out something clever. They didn't need to make money on toys. They could sell the hottest toys at cost, or even at a loss, just to get parents through the door. Once you were inside, you'd buy groceries, clothes and everything else.

[VISUAL: two bars racing; numbers count up: "17.4%" vs "16.8%". Caption: "1998".]
Toys"R"Us couldn't do that. Toys were all it sold. In nineteen ninety eight, for the first time in more than a decade, it lost its crown. Walmart sold seventeen point four percent of America's toys. Toys"R"Us sold sixteen point eight {F6}.

[VISUAL: half-empty aisle, a lonely employee.]
It was still a giant. But now it was a giant with a problem. And its solution to the next problem would turn out even worse.

## 4:35 — The Amazon mistake
[VISUAL: early 2000s computer, dial-up era website loading. Caption: "2000".]
In nineteen ninety nine, the internet was exploding, and Toys"R"Us had a disaster on its hands. That Christmas, its website couldn't keep up with orders. So in two thousand, it made a choice that sounds unbelievable today.

[VISUAL: two logos shaking hands (generic boxes, no real logos); stamp "10 YEARS".]
It signed a ten year deal with Amazon {F8}. Toys"R"Us would become the exclusive toy seller on Amazon's site. In plain English: it handed its online store to the company that would one day eat the entire retail world.

[VISUAL: courtroom gavel; caption "2004 → 2006".]
For a few years, it worked. Then Amazon started letting other sellers list toys too. Toys"R"Us felt betrayed, and in two thousand four, it sued. In two thousand six, a judge let it walk away from the deal {F8}.

[VISUAL: calendar pages flying; kinetic: "LOST YEARS".]
It won in court. But it lost something it could never get back: almost a decade of learning how to sell online. Years later, Amazon paid fifty one million dollars to settle the fight {F8}. By then, Amazon was the future. And Toys"R"Us was still figuring out its own website.

[VISUAL: subscribe moment, giraffe silhouette waving in a dark aisle.]
If you grew up in those aisles and you want more stories like this, the toys, the stores and the brands that disappeared, subscribe. There's a lot more coming.

## 6:00 — The deal that doomed it
[VISUAL: glass boardroom, three silhouettes, contract on the table. Caption: "March 2005".]
Now we get to the real answer. In March of two thousand five, three investment firms, Bain Capital, KKR and the real estate company Vornado, agreed to buy Toys"R"Us for six point six billion dollars {F9}.

[VISUAL: diagram: small block "their money" on top of a huge block "borrowed".]
But here's the catch. Most of that money wasn't theirs. It was borrowed. It's called a leveraged buyout. And in a leveraged buyout, the debt doesn't stay with the buyers. It gets loaded onto the company they just bought.

[VISUAL: a chain and a weight attached to a shopping cart.]
Overnight, Toys"R"Us went from a struggling company to a struggling company carrying around five billion dollars of debt {F10}.

[VISUAL: stack of cash burning; number counts up: "$400,000,000 / year".]
Just the interest on that debt cost about four hundred million dollars a year {F10}. Every single year. Before paying a single employee. Before fixing a single store.

[VISUAL: peeling paint, broken shelf, flickering light.]
That's money that could have gone into better stores, a better website, better prices. Instead, it went to the lenders. The stores started to look tired. The shelves got messier. The website stayed clunky. And customers noticed.

[VISUAL: Times Square store going dark. Caption: "Dec 30, 2015".]
In December of two thousand fifteen, even the Times Square flagship closed its doors {F7}. The rent there was reported at around forty two million dollars a year. The company simply couldn't afford its own crown jewel anymore.

[VISUAL: dark aisle; kinetic: "THEN…"]
Then came the moment everyone knew was coming.

## 7:50 — The fall
[VISUAL: newspaper-style headline; stamp "CHAPTER 11". Caption: "September 2017".]
In September of two thousand seventeen, Toys"R"Us filed for bankruptcy {F10}. The plan was to restructure, survive Christmas, and come back stronger.

[VISUAL: holiday season, thin crowds, "SALE" signs.]
But Christmas went badly. Sales fell short. Suppliers got nervous. The lenders lost patience.

[VISUAL: back to the opening image: store at night; caption "March 15, 2018".]
And on March fifteenth, twenty eighteen, the company announced it would liquidate. Every store in America would close {F3}.

[VISUAL: elderly man's silhouette by a window, out of focus; candle. No real face generated.]
Exactly one week later, on March twenty second, Charles Lazarus died at the age of ninety four {F1}{F2}. The man who had turned a crib shop into the biggest toy store in the world lived just long enough to see it end.

[VISUAL: "STORE CLOSING" banners, empty shelves, one plush toy left behind.]
The liquidation sales lasted a few months. Shelves emptied. Signs came down. By the end of June, the last stores had locked their doors {F3}.

[VISUAL: employees in vests hugging, name tags; kinetic: "$0 SEVERANCE".]
Around thirty thousand workers lost their jobs {F3}. And because of how bankruptcy works, they didn't get severance. Later that year, after public pressure, two of the owners set up a twenty million dollar hardship fund for former employees {F11}. For many workers, it was far less than what they had lost.

[VISUAL: dark aisle; kinetic: "WAS IT AMAZON?"]
So, was it Amazon? Was it Walmart?

## 9:40 — What really killed it
[VISUAL: three cards side by side: price tag, computer screen, chain with a weight.]
The honest answer is: all three, but not equally. Walmart took its customers. The Amazon deal cost it the internet. But plenty of retailers survived Walmart and Amazon. Target did. Best Buy did.

[VISUAL: the weight crashes down; kinetic: "THE DEBT".]
What Toys"R"Us couldn't survive was competing against both of them with four hundred million dollars a year walking out the door. The debt didn't just hurt the company. It took away its chance to fight back.

## 10:20 — The ghost comes back
[VISUAL: Macy's-style department store with a colorful toy shop inside. Caption: "2021".]
And that's where the story should end. But it didn't.

[VISUAL: a brand name on a contract changing hands; then airport terminal with a toy shop.]
The brand was sold, and sold again. Its new owner, WHP Global, started putting Toys"R"Us shops inside Macy's stores. Then a flagship store. Then shops in airports {F4}.

[VISUAL: new bright store, café, kids and adults shopping. Kinetic: "160 STORES".]
And this year, in twenty twenty six, Toys"R"Us announced one hundred and twenty new standalone stores, bringing its total in the US to around one hundred and sixty {F4}. A lot of the shoppers they're chasing now aren't kids. They're adults. The same kids who sang the jingle, now buying toys for their own children, and for themselves.

[VISUAL: old store photo dissolves into the new store.]
It's not the same company. The old Toys"R"Us is gone. What's coming back is the name, the giraffe, and the memory.

[VISUAL: kid's hand holding a paper ticket in the video game aisle; slow fade.]
But maybe that's what Toys"R"Us really sold all along. Not toys. The feeling of walking through those doors when you were seven years old.

[VISUAL: end screen.]
So tell me in the comments: what was the first toy you remember buying at Toys"R"Us? And if you want to know what happened to the toy that adults thought would make them rich, watch the Beanie Babies story next.
