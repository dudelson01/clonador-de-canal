## 0:00 — Hook
[VISUAL: older man stops halfway up a staircase, hand tight on the rail.]
After sixty, you can lose a little muscle every single year without noticing. Nothing hurts. Nothing changes overnight. Until one day, the stairs feel a little taller than they used to. Getting up from a low chair takes two tries. And a bag of groceries feels heavier than it should.

[VISUAL: supermarket fridge aisle, slow push-in to a plain tub of cottage cheese.]
Today, I want to show you one of the cheapest foods in the supermarket fridge. It's not a supplement, it's not a powder, and you've probably walked past it a hundred times. One cup of it has about as much protein as four eggs. And the moment of the day when you eat it may matter more than you think.

[VISUAL: title card "Silver Plate Nutrition".]
Quick note before we start: this video is general education, not medical advice. All sources are in the description. If you have kidney disease or take medication, talk to your doctor before changing your diet.

## 0:45 — The muscle you lose without noticing
[3D: thigh muscle slowly thinning.] [CADERNO: "3–5% per decade".]
Doctors have a name for this slow loss: sarcopenia. It's the loss of muscle mass and strength that comes with age. According to Harvard Health, most of us start losing muscle around age thirty, at about three to five percent every decade {F3}. Some estimates go as high as eight percent per decade {F4}.

[CADERNO: hand-drawn line that bends down more steeply after 60.]
That sounds small. But it adds up. And here's the part most people don't hear: the loss tends to speed up after sixty {F4}{F5}. The muscles that suffer first are often the big ones in your legs, the ones you use to stand up, climb stairs and keep your balance.

[VISUAL: older woman gripping a railing; then a grandchild running toward her.]
Strong legs aren't about looking fit. They're about independence: getting out of a chair on your own, carrying your groceries, playing with your grandchildren, and avoiding falls.

But it wasn't the lack of exercise that surprised researchers the most. It was something on the breakfast table.

## 1:55 — The protein your breakfast is missing
[CADERNO: three plates — breakfast, lunch, dinner — with grams written above each.]
Muscle is built and repaired from protein. And older adults need more of it than younger people, not less. A group of international experts, called the PROT-AGE Study Group, recommends that people over sixty-five eat at least 1.0 to 1.2 grams of protein per kilogram of body weight every day {F1}. For someone who weighs seventy kilos, that's roughly seventy to eighty-four grams a day.

[CADERNO: "Breakfast: ~10–13 g".]
Now look at how many older adults actually eat. In the Newcastle 85+ Study in England, breakfast was the meal with the least protein of the whole day: around ten grams for women and thirteen for men {F6}. Dinner had two to three times more.

[CADERNO: a "switch" drawn in ink that only flips on at dinner.]
Why does that matter? Because research suggests your muscles respond best when a meal has about twenty-five to thirty grams of quality protein {F2}. Below roughly twenty grams, the muscle-building response in older adults tends to be weaker {F2}. So if breakfast is toast and coffee, and lunch is light, your muscles may get only one real chance a day to rebuild: dinner.

That's where today's food comes in. But first, you need to understand something strange that happens to muscles as we age.

## 3:15 — What happens inside your body
[3D: cottage cheese entering the stomach, glowing particles spreading.]
Let's follow a spoonful of cottage cheese after you swallow it.

Most of the protein in cottage cheese is casein, a milk protein. When casein reaches your stomach, it forms soft clumps, and your body breaks it down slowly. So instead of arriving all at once, the building blocks of protein, called amino acids, drip into your bloodstream over several hours.

[3D: amino acids flowing through blood vessels toward a thigh muscle.]
Those amino acids travel to your muscles. And some of them work like a signal, a message that says: it's time to repair and rebuild.

[3D: the muscle "receiving" the signal weakly, faint flicker.]
But here's the strange part. As we get older, muscles become less sensitive to that signal. Scientists call it anabolic resistance {F10}. It's a bit like a phone with weak reception: the call is coming in, but the muscle doesn't pick up as easily as it did at thirty.

[CADERNO: two arrows, "more protein per meal" and "movement", both pointing to a muscle.]
The good news is that researchers have found two ways to boost the signal. The first is getting enough protein in a single meal, instead of small amounts spread thin {F10}. The second is moving your muscles close to the time you eat, even with simple exercises {F10}. Keep both in mind, because they come back in a moment.

## 4:45 — Cottage cheese vs. eggs
[VISUAL: bowl of cottage cheese in close-up; four eggs slide in beside it with a "=" sign.]
Cottage cheese is a soft, fresh cheese made from milk curds. It's mild, it's cheap, and it's one of the most protein-dense foods you can buy.

[CADERNO: "1 cup = 24 g protein" / "1 egg = 6.3 g".]
According to the U.S. Department of Agriculture, one cup of low-fat cottage cheese, about two hundred and twenty grams, has around twenty-four grams of protein {F7}. A large egg has about six point three grams {F8}. So one cup of cottage cheese gives you roughly the same protein as four eggs.

And remember the number from a moment ago: twenty-five to thirty grams per meal. One cup of cottage cheese gets you almost there, in a single bowl, without cooking anything.

[VISUAL: slow-motion spoon lifting cottage cheese.]
Because casein digests slowly, it also keeps you full for a long time. That can help if your appetite is smaller than it used to be.

[VISUAL: shopping basket, price-tag icon, no number.]
And it's usually an affordable way to get protein. Next time you shop, compare the price per gram of protein with meat, fish or protein powder. Cottage cheese often wins.

Does that mean eggs are bad? Not at all. Eggs are excellent. The point is simple: if you only eat one egg at breakfast, you're getting about six grams. Cottage cheese makes it much easier to reach the amount your muscles actually respond to.

## 6:15 — Four myths about protein after 60
[CADERNO: page titled "Myths", four numbered lines.]
Before we get to the recipes, let's clear up four myths that keep a lot of people from eating enough protein.

[CADERNO: "Myth 1: you need less as you age" crossed out.]
Myth number one: you need less protein as you get older. It's the opposite. The general recommendation for adults is about 0.8 grams per kilo, but experts on aging recommend at least 1.0 to 1.2 for people over sixty-five {F1}{F10}. Your body needs more, not less, to hold on to the muscle it has.

[CADERNO: kidney sketch with a question mark.]
Myth number two: protein damages healthy kidneys. For older adults with healthy kidneys, the PROT-AGE experts consider 1.0 to 1.2 grams per kilo appropriate {F1}. The exception is people with significant kidney disease, who may need less, and that's a conversation to have with your doctor {F1}.

[CADERNO: steak, egg, cheese, beans drawn side by side.]
Myth number three: only meat has good protein. Dairy, eggs, fish and beans are all useful sources. Cottage cheese is one of the easiest, because it's soft, ready to eat and doesn't need much chewing or a stove.

[3D: muscle lighting up as a person stands up from a chair.]
Myth number four: if you eat enough protein, the muscle will come back by itself. Food gives your muscles the materials. Movement tells them to use those materials. Remember the weak signal? Researchers found that the response to protein improves when people are active close to their meals {F10}.

## 7:45 — Four cheap ways to eat it
[VISUAL: each recipe shown top-down, ingredients appearing one by one.]
Here are four simple ways to eat it.

Number one: the sweet bowl. Half a cup to a cup of cottage cheese, a sliced banana or a handful of berries, and a spoon of oats or chopped nuts. It takes one minute and tastes like dessert.

Number two: the savory toast. Spread cottage cheese on a slice of whole-grain toast, add sliced tomato, black pepper and a drizzle of olive oil. Add a boiled egg on the side and you've passed thirty grams of protein.

Number three: the fluffy eggs. Stir two or three spoons of cottage cheese into your scrambled eggs right at the end. They get creamier, and you add protein without anyone noticing.

[VISUAL: blender with cottage cheese, banana and milk.]
Number four: the smoothie. If chewing is difficult or your appetite is low in the morning, blend half a cup of cottage cheese with a banana and a glass of milk. It comes out thick, creamy and easy to drink.

[CADERNO: "Tip: spread protein across the day".]
One more tip: don't save all your protein for dinner. A protein-rich breakfast, a decent lunch and a normal dinner is the pattern researchers suggest works better for muscle {F2}.

## 9:00 — What about at night?
[VISUAL: dim kitchen at night, small bowl on the table, warm lamp.]
There's one more moment of the day worth knowing about: before bed.

[CADERNO: "48 men · average age 72 · 40 g before sleep".]
In a study from Maastricht University, researchers gave older men, with an average age of seventy-two, a casein drink before sleep {F9}. With forty grams of casein, their muscles built more protein during the night than with a placebo. But with twenty grams, the effect wasn't clear {F9}.

[CADERNO: "1 cup cottage cheese ≈ 24 g" next to "study: 40 g".]
To be honest about it: this study used a drink, not cottage cheese, and the effective amount was larger than one cup. So it doesn't prove that a bowl of cottage cheese at night will build muscle. What it does show is that the night is not wasted time for your muscles, and that slow-digesting protein, like the casein in cottage cheese, can be used while you sleep. If you like an evening snack, a small bowl of cottage cheese is a far better choice than cookies.

## 9:50 — Who should be careful
[VISUAL: salt shaker; then [3D] kidney with a caution sign.]
Now, the honest part. Cottage cheese isn't perfect for everyone.

First: salt. The same cup that gives you twenty-four grams of protein can have around seven hundred milligrams of sodium or more {F7}. If you have high blood pressure, look for low-sodium or no-salt-added versions, and keep the portion to half a cup.

Second: kidneys. As we saw, older adults with significant kidney disease may need to limit protein {F1}. If you have kidney disease, don't increase your protein without talking to your doctor.

Third: lactose. Cottage cheese has less lactose than milk, but some people still feel it. Lactose-free versions exist in many stores.

## 10:50 — Summary
[CADERNO: three check marks appearing one by one.]
So let's recap.
One: after sixty, muscle loss tends to speed up, and older muscles respond less to protein, so every meal counts.
Two: most older adults eat the least protein at breakfast, when their muscles could use it.
Three: one cup of cottage cheese has about as much protein as four eggs, costs little, needs no cooking, and works even better when you move your body.
