## 0:00 — Hook
[VISUAL: older man stops halfway up a staircase, hand on the rail. Slow zoom.]
After sixty, you can lose a little muscle every single year without noticing. Nothing hurts. Nothing changes overnight. Until one day, the stairs feel a little taller than they used to. Getting up from a low chair takes two tries. And a bag of groceries feels heavier than it should.

[VISUAL: supermarket fridge aisle, camera glides to a plain tub of cottage cheese.]
Today, I want to show you one of the cheapest foods in the supermarket fridge. It's not a supplement, it's not a powder, and you've probably walked past it a hundred times. One cup of it has about as much protein as four eggs. And the moment of the day when you eat it may matter more than you think.

[VISUAL: title card "Silver Plate Nutrition".]
Quick note before we start: this video is general education, not medical advice. All sources are in the description. If you have kidney disease or take medication, talk to your doctor before changing your diet.

## 0:50 — The muscle you lose without noticing
[VISUAL: 3D animation of a thigh muscle slowly getting thinner; on-screen text "3–5% per decade".]
Doctors have a name for this slow loss: sarcopenia. It's age-related loss of muscle mass and strength. According to Harvard Health, most of us start losing muscle around age thirty, at about three to five percent every decade {F3}. Some estimates go as high as eight percent per decade {F4}.

[VISUAL: line chart that curves down more steeply after 60.]
That sounds small. But it adds up. And here's the part most people don't hear: the loss tends to speed up after sixty {F4}{F5}. The muscles that suffer first are often the big ones in your legs, the ones you use to stand up, climb stairs and keep your balance.

[VISUAL: older woman holding a railing, then a grandchild running toward her.]
Strong legs aren't about looking fit. They're about independence: getting out of a chair on your own, carrying your groceries, playing with your grandchildren, and avoiding falls.

But it wasn't the lack of exercise that surprised researchers the most. It was something on the breakfast table.

## 2:10 — The protein your breakfast is missing
[VISUAL: three plates side by side — breakfast, lunch, dinner — with protein grams appearing over each.]
Muscle is built and repaired from protein. And older adults need more of it than younger people, not less. A group of international experts, called the PROT-AGE Study Group, recommends that people over sixty-five eat at least 1.0 to 1.2 grams of protein per kilogram of body weight every day {F1}. For someone who weighs seventy kilos, that's roughly seventy to eighty-four grams a day.

[VISUAL: on-screen text "Breakfast: ~10–13 g".]
Now look at how most older adults actually eat. In the Newcastle 85+ Study in England, breakfast was the meal with the least protein of the whole day: around ten grams for women and thirteen for men {F6}. Dinner had two to three times more.

[VISUAL: animation of a "muscle-building switch" turning on only at dinner.]
Why does that matter? Because research suggests your muscles respond best when a meal has about twenty-five to thirty grams of quality protein {F2}. Below roughly twenty grams, the muscle-building response in older adults tends to be weaker {F2}. So if breakfast is toast and coffee, and lunch is light, your muscles may get only one real chance a day to rebuild: dinner.

That's where today's food comes in.

## 3:50 — Cottage cheese vs. eggs
[VISUAL: bowl of cottage cheese in close-up, then 4 eggs appear beside it with a "=" sign.]
Cottage cheese is a soft, fresh cheese made from milk curds. It's mild, it's cheap, and it's one of the most protein-dense foods you can buy.

[VISUAL: on-screen text "1 cup = 24 g protein" / "1 egg = 6.3 g".]
According to the U.S. Department of Agriculture, one cup of low-fat cottage cheese, about two hundred and twenty grams, has around twenty-four grams of protein {F7}. A large egg has about six point three grams {F8}. So one cup of cottage cheese gives you roughly the same protein as four eggs.

And remember the number from a moment ago: twenty-five to thirty grams per meal. One cup of cottage cheese gets you almost there, in a single bowl, without cooking anything.

[VISUAL: slow-motion spoon lifting cottage cheese.]
Most of the protein in cottage cheese is casein, a milk protein the body digests slowly. That's one reason it keeps you full for a long time, which can help if your appetite is smaller than it used to be.

[VISUAL: shopping basket; price tag icon, no specific number.]
And it's usually an affordable way to get protein. Next time you shop, compare the price per gram of protein with meat, fish or protein powder. Cottage cheese often wins.

Does that mean eggs are bad? Not at all. Eggs are excellent. The point is simple: if you only eat one egg at breakfast, you're getting about six grams. Cottage cheese makes it much easier to reach the amount your muscles actually respond to.

## 5:40 — Three cheap breakfasts
[VISUAL: each recipe shown top-down, ingredients appearing one by one.]
Here are three simple ways to eat it in the morning.

Number one: the sweet bowl. Half a cup to a cup of cottage cheese, a sliced banana or a handful of berries, and a spoon of oats or chopped nuts. It takes one minute and tastes like dessert.

Number two: the savory toast. Spread cottage cheese on a slice of whole-grain toast, add sliced tomato, black pepper and a drizzle of olive oil. Add a boiled egg on the side and you've passed thirty grams of protein.

Number three: the fluffy eggs. Stir two or three spoons of cottage cheese into your scrambled eggs right at the end. They get creamier, and you add protein without anyone noticing.

[VISUAL: on-screen text "Tip: spread protein across the day".]
One more tip: don't save all your protein for dinner. A protein-rich breakfast, a decent lunch and a normal dinner is the pattern that researchers suggest works better for muscle {F2}.

And food is only half of the story. Muscles grow back when you also use them. Even simple exercises, like standing up from a chair ten times, can make a big difference. Ask your doctor or a physical therapist which exercises are safe for you.

## 7:00 — Who should be careful
[VISUAL: salt shaker; then a kidney icon with a caution sign.]
Now, the honest part. Cottage cheese isn't perfect for everyone.

First: salt. The same cup that gives you twenty-four grams of protein can have around seven hundred milligrams of sodium or more {F7}. If you have high blood pressure, look for low-sodium or no-salt-added versions, and keep the portion to half a cup.

Second: kidneys. The PROT-AGE experts make a clear exception for older adults with severe kidney disease who are not on dialysis: they may need to limit protein {F1}. If you have kidney disease, don't increase your protein without talking to your doctor.

Third: lactose. Cottage cheese has less lactose than milk, but some people still feel it. Lactose-free versions exist in many stores.

## 7:50 — Summary
[VISUAL: three checkmarks appearing one by one.]
So let's recap.
One: after sixty, muscle loss tends to speed up, and it often starts in the legs.
Two: most older adults eat the least protein at breakfast, when their muscles could use it.
Three: one cup of cottage cheese has about as much protein as four eggs, costs little, and takes no cooking.
