## Hook
[VISUAL: older woman standing up from a dining chair without using her hands.]
Your legs carry you through every single day. Out of bed, up the stairs, into the car, across the supermarket. And after sixty, they're often the first part of the body to quietly lose strength.

[VISUAL: supermarket aisle with an ordinary shopping basket.]
The good news? You don't need expensive powders or fancy supplements to fight back. Today I'll show you seven cheap foods from an ordinary grocery list that can help your leg muscles. And number seven isn't even a protein food. In a study of almost four thousand people, it was linked to eleven percent stronger legs.

[VISUAL: title card.]
Quick note: this video is general education, not medical advice. All sources are in the description. If you have kidney disease or take medication, talk to your doctor before changing your diet.

## Why the legs come first
[3D: legs of an older person, large thigh muscles glowing.]
The biggest muscles in your body are in your legs, and they're the ones you use to stand up, walk and keep your balance. With age, we slowly lose muscle, a process called sarcopenia, and the legs feel it first.

[CADERNO: a plate with "25–30 g".]
To protect those muscles, researchers recommend that older adults eat more protein than younger people, not less: at least 1.0 to 1.2 grams per kilo of body weight a day {F1}. And it seems to work best when a meal has about twenty-five to thirty grams of protein {F2}. So let's see seven cheap ways to get there.

## A 30-second test for your legs
[VISUAL: a sturdy dining chair against a wall, a kitchen timer next to it.]
Before we start, here's a simple way to check your own leg strength at home. It's called the thirty-second chair stand test, and it's part of a fall-prevention program from the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention {F12}.

[VISUAL: older man sitting in the middle of a chair, arms crossed over his chest.]
Sit in the middle of a sturdy chair placed against a wall, feet flat on the floor, arms crossed over your chest. Then count how many times you can stand up fully and sit back down in thirty seconds. Only do this if you can stand safely, and have someone next to you the first time.

[CADERNO: a small table "60–64: men 14 · women 12 / 65–69: 12 · 11 / 70–74: 12 · 10".]
According to the CDC, a score below average suggests a higher risk of falls. Below fourteen stands for men and twelve for women between sixty and sixty-four. Below twelve for men and eleven for women between sixty-five and sixty-nine. And below twelve for men and ten for women between seventy and seventy-four {F12}.

[VISUAL: older woman writing a number on a notepad on the fridge.]
Write your number down. Then repeat the test in a couple of months, after adding these foods and a little daily movement, and see if it goes up.

## Number one: eggs
[VISUAL: a carton of brown eggs on a kitchen counter.]
Number one: eggs. One large egg has about six point three grams of high-quality protein {F3}. They're cheap, they keep for weeks, and they cook in minutes.

[CADERNO: two eggs plus a glass of milk, "≈ 20 g".]
The catch is that one egg alone isn't enough for a full meal of protein. Two eggs with a glass of milk, or eggs with beans or cheese, gets you much closer to that twenty-five gram target.

[VISUAL: scrambled eggs with spinach on a plate.]
A simple idea: scramble two eggs with a handful of spinach. You'll see in a moment why the spinach matters. And if you like boiled eggs, cook six at once on Sunday. You'll have a ready-made protein snack in the fridge for the whole week.

## Number two: canned sardines
[VISUAL: an open can of sardines on a wooden board with lemon.]
Number two: canned sardines. They're one of the cheapest sources of animal protein in the supermarket. A small can, about seventy-five grams drained, has around eighteen grams of protein {F4}. And because you usually eat the soft bones, you also get calcium.

[3D: omega-3 particles flowing into a thigh muscle.]
Sardines are also rich in omega-3 fats. In a six-month study, healthy adults aged sixty to eighty-five took fish oil every day. Compared with a placebo, their thigh muscle volume increased by three point six percent, and their grip strength went up by about two kilos {F5}.

[CADERNO: a fish-oil capsule next to a sardine, "study used capsules".]
To be fair, that study used fish oil capsules, not sardines, and at a fairly high dose. But it shows that the fats in oily fish may do more for your muscles than most people think. Try sardines on whole-grain toast, or mashed with lemon and onion. Just look for low-sodium versions if you watch your blood pressure. Two cans a week is an easy, affordable goal.

## Number three: lentils and beans
[VISUAL: a pot of lentil soup steaming on the stove.]
Number three: lentils and beans. They're probably the cheapest protein on this list. One cup of cooked lentils has about eighteen grams of protein {F6}, plus fiber, which also helps with digestion.

[CADERNO: a bowl of lentils next to a bowl of rice.]
Combining them with grains, like lentils with rice or beans with corn, gives you a more complete protein. And a big pot of lentil soup can feed you for several days for very little money. If cooking takes too long, canned beans work too. Just rinse them under water to wash away some of the salt.

## Number four: Greek yogurt
[VISUAL: a bowl of thick Greek yogurt with honey and walnuts.]
Number four: plain Greek yogurt. Because it's strained, it has much more protein than regular yogurt: around seventeen to twenty grams in one cup, depending on the brand {F7}. It's soft, it needs no cooking, and it's easy to eat even when your appetite is small.

[VISUAL: hand reading the label of a yogurt tub, label blurred.]
One tip: buy the plain version and add your own fruit or a little honey. Flavored yogurts often have a lot of added sugar. A cup of Greek yogurt with berries in the afternoon is one of the simplest ways to add a protein meal to your day.

## Number five: milk
[VISUAL: a glass of milk being poured at breakfast.]
Number five: milk. One cup has about eight grams of protein {F8}, and it's one of the easiest ways to add protein to a meal without cooking anything: in your coffee, in oatmeal, or in a smoothie.

[CADERNO: a sun and a glass of milk with a question mark, "vitamin D?".]
In many countries, milk is fortified with vitamin D, which your muscles and bones need. But here's an honest note: in large studies, vitamin D pills didn't make muscles stronger or prevent falls in older adults who already had enough of it {F9}. So don't take big doses on your own. Ask your doctor to check your levels instead. And if milk upsets your stomach, lactose-free milk has the same protein.

## Number six: peanut butter
[VISUAL: a spoon of peanut butter over a jar, close-up.]
Number six: peanut butter. Two tablespoons have about seven grams of protein {F10}. It's cheap, it lasts for months, and it makes almost anything more filling.

[VISUAL: whole-grain toast with peanut butter and banana slices.]
Choose a peanut butter whose ingredients are just peanuts and maybe salt. And keep an eye on the portion: it's high in calories, so two tablespoons is a good amount. On whole-grain toast with a sliced banana, it's a quick breakfast or snack. It also works in oatmeal, or as a dip for apple slices.

## Number seven: leafy greens
[VISUAL: a pile of fresh spinach, lettuce and a beetroot on a table.]
And number seven, the one that surprised researchers: leafy green vegetables, like spinach, lettuce and kale. They're not a protein food. But they're rich in natural nitrates.

[CADERNO: "3,759 people · 12 years · +11% leg strength".]
A study from Edith Cowan University in Australia followed three thousand seven hundred and fifty-nine people for twelve years. Those who ate the most nitrate-rich vegetables had eleven percent stronger lower-limb strength than those who ate the least, and walked up to four percent faster {F11}.

[3D: blood vessels widening and more blood flowing to the leg muscles.]
This was an observational study, so it shows a link, not proof. But the researchers themselves suggested the best combination: a diet rich in leafy greens, plus regular exercise {F11}. And a handful of spinach costs almost nothing. Add it to eggs, soups, sandwiches or smoothies. You'll barely taste it, but your legs may thank you.

## A cheap day of protein
[CADERNO: a timeline of the day with four small plates.]
So how does it all fit together? Here's a simple day.

[VISUAL: breakfast table with two eggs, spinach and a glass of milk.]
Breakfast: two eggs scrambled with spinach and a glass of milk. That's about twenty grams of protein.

[VISUAL: bowl of lentil soup with bread.]
Lunch: a bowl of lentils, about eighteen grams.

[VISUAL: Greek yogurt with fruit as an afternoon snack.]
Afternoon: a cup of Greek yogurt with fruit, around seventeen to twenty grams.

[VISUAL: dinner plate with sardines on toast and a green salad.]
Dinner: a can of sardines with a big green salad, around eighteen grams.

[CADERNO: "≈ 75 g protein".]
That's roughly seventy-five grams of protein in one day, from cheap, simple foods. For someone who weighs seventy kilos, that's right inside the range experts recommend {F1}.

## Who should be careful
[VISUAL: kidneys in 3D with a soft warning glow.]
Now, the honest part. If you have kidney disease, you may need to limit protein and some minerals found in dairy, beans and nuts. Please follow your doctor's advice before eating more of these foods {F1}.

[VISUAL: pill organizer next to a bowl of spinach.]
If you take a blood thinner such as warfarin, talk to your doctor before you suddenly eat a lot more leafy greens. Their vitamin K can affect how the medicine works.

[VISUAL: peanuts and a can of fish.]
And of course, skip peanuts if you're allergic, and choose low-sodium canned fish if you have high blood pressure.

## Summary
[CADERNO: seven small drawings in a row.]
Let's recap the seven foods: eggs, canned sardines, lentils and beans, Greek yogurt, milk, peanut butter, and leafy greens. All cheap, all easy to find.

[3D: leg muscles glowing as a person walks.]
And remember: food gives your muscles the materials, but movement tells them to use them. Even standing up from a chair ten times a day helps. Ask your doctor or a physical therapist which exercises are safe for you.

Which of these seven do you already eat every week? Tell me in the comments.
